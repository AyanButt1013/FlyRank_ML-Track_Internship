Predicting Content Decay & Ranking Signals in Enterprise Search Portfolios

Research Staff / ML Engineering Intern
Ranking Signal Analysis & Actionable Content Playbooks
FlyRank Enterprise Warehouse (79M+ Impression Events)

Abstract—Organic search visibility for enterprise content portfolios decays non-linearly over time, yet traditional search engine optimization relies on static age-based heuristics to schedule content updates. This study investigates whether tree-based machine learning models can accurately identify organic impression decay across enterprise search domains prior to traffic loss. We constructed a 75/25 temporal split over 148,000 active content URLs to train a Random Forest classifier evaluated via GroupKFold cross-validation by client domain. When evaluated on unseen client domains, the model achieved a 0.5032 PR-AUC and 0.5383 ROC-AUC, demonstrating a measured −0.0967 performance drop compared to naive un-grouped splits due to client-level domain authority leakage. Based on these findings, we deployed an actionable decision-support playbook mapping model probabilities to reason codes, producing a triage queue of 120,727 content items while establishing strict human-review guardrails against unverified automated edits.

Index Terms—content decay, search engine optimization, Random Forest, GroupKFold, data leakage, ranking signals, editorial playbook

I. Introduction & Problem Statement

Enterprise digital publishing platforms maintain thousands of historical assets spanning established evergreen articles, competitive product landing pages, and long-tail informational guides. Over time, search engine algorithms, shifting user intent, and competitor activity induce non-linear traffic decay.

Conventional content operational playbooks rely heavily on rule-based heuristics—such as flagging any article older than 180 days for a mandatory rewrite. However, linear rules fail to capture non-linear feature interactions, such as high-impression evergreen pages maintaining top SERP rankings despite high content age, or low-word-count pages suffering rapid click drops due to SERP feature compression. This paper formulates content decay prediction as a binary classification problem and evaluates machine learning performance under strict domain-grouped validation.

II. Data Framing & Scope

The dataset originates from the FlyRank Enterprise Warehouse dataset, capturing daily Google Search Console (GSC) performance logs and content metadata across 57 distinct client domains.

  • Total Records Analyzed: 148,133 active content items (≥ 10 impressions).
  • Feature Observation Window: Historical 90-day aggregated window (t0 → t1).
  • Forward Target Window: Subsequent 30-day window (t1 → t2).
  • Data Safety & Exclusions: All internal product decision flags (such as health_score or priority_score) were strictly excluded from model feature vectors to ensure zero target leakage. All client hashes and URL paths remain anonymized.

A. Model Feature Vector (X)

The feature space consists of six observable historical signals:

  1. days_since_update: Content age in days relative to dataset snapshot.
  2. word_count_clean: Imputed page word count.
  3. impressions_90d: Total GSC search impressions.
  4. clicks_90d: Total GSC organic clicks.
  5. avg_position: Average SERP ranking position.
  6. ctr: Historical click-through rate,
    ctr = clicks / impressions (2)

B. Validation Split Strategy

To prevent client-level domain authority and industry niche traits from leaking across validation folds, we employed a 5-fold GroupKFold cross-validation grouped by client_hash_id. This guarantees that test performance is evaluated exclusively on unseen client domains.

A. Error Analysis Highlights

False positives (predicted decline, actual stable) occur primarily on high-authority evergreen domain pages where high content age (≥ 180 days) triggers risk flags despite ranking stability.

False negatives (predicted stable, actual decline) occur when high historical 90-day impression volume masks a recent subtle ranking shift from position 3.2 to position 8.5.

A. Human Review Guardrails & the “No-Go” List

The following actions are strictly prohibited from automated execution:

  1. Automated content rewriting / deletion: LLM agents must never automatically overwrite or delete live URLs without editorial verification.
  2. Core revenue assets: Pages generating > 10% of client sales conversions require senior editorial sign-off.
  3. Canonical URL modifications: Slug alterations that alter established canonical links.

VII. Limitations & Honest Framing

This study is observational; correlations between content freshness, word count, and impression performance do not imply direct causation. Model predictions serve as a directional decision-support triage tool for editorial teams, rather than a deterministic ranking guarantee.
